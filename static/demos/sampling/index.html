What this simulation assumes

These students and their social-media use are fictional. Each student has a fixed daily usage time, recorded to the nearest tenth of a minute. For the sampling model, 40% are lower-use students (0–60 minutes), 40% moderate-use (60–180), and 20% higher-use (180–360). These are illustrative categories, not standard cutoffs. The population average is approximately 108 minutes per day.

Each sample selects students without replacement. A student may be selected again in a later sample; repeated samples are not necessarily different people. Every plotted dot summarizes one complete sample. Clicking it restores the exact observations that produced it. Changing the displayed variable recalculates statistics for those same students; it does not draw new samples.

The convenience example assumes that recruitment on social media disproportionately reaches heavier users. It illustrates one possible selection mechanism, not all social-media recruitment or convenience sampling. Everyone selected responds accurately; nonresponse and measurement error are not simulated.

Each fictional student also has a fixed class year and gender. The campus is 30% first-year students, 26% sophomores, 22% juniors, and 22% seniors; 55% women, 42% men, and 3% nonbinary students. In this invented population, heavier users are more often first-year students, while the gender proportions are the same across usage groups. Recruitment can therefore distort the class-year mix while approximately matching gender. Matching these demographics alone does not establish that the sample is representative on social-media use.

For social-media use, all plots use the same 0–360 minutes-per-day scale. For class year or gender, the upper panel highlights students in the selected category; its percentage marker and every repeated-sample plot use a 0–100% scale. Only that category’s percentage is plotted, not an average of category codes. Points are stacked in narrow bins for legibility; statistics use the actual observations. Dot size and stack height adapt to the available space, so compare horizontal spread and location rather than stack height across panels.
